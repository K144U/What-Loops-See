## 1. The main question now has evidence

The project asks what determines how many "thinking loops" one of these
models needs. Our prediction was that longer reasoning chains need more
loops, and that a merely busier picture does not.

We can now show the first half of that. The table below is accuracy on
questions of different chain lengths, at different numbers of loops. A
chain of length 4 means a question like "what colour is the object above
the object left of the object below the red square", four linked steps.

| chain length | 1 loop | 2 loops | 3 loops | 4 loops | 8 loops |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 100% | 100% | 100% | 100% | 100% |
| 2 | 99% | 100% | 100% | 100% | 100% |
| 3 | 92% | 100% | 100% | 100% | 100% |
| 4 | 79% | 99% | 100% | 100% | 100% |
| 5 | 73% | 95% | 100% | 100% | 100% |
| 6 | 60% | 89% | 98% | 100% | 100% |

Read down any column: the longer the chain, the worse the model does. Read
across any row: more loops fix it. Guessing scores 18%.

That is the shape the hypothesis predicts, and it is the first time we have
seen it. The equivalent table a week ago was 100% in every single cell.

**One detail worth having.** Before running this we wrote down, in advance,
a specific numerical prediction for how many loops each chain length would
need: 1, 1, 1, 2, 3, 4. What we measured was 1, 1, 1, 2, 2, 3. The first
four are exactly right, and the last two are one better than predicted, so
the model is slightly more efficient per loop than our model of it. We are
reporting the prediction as we made it rather than adjusting it afterwards.

**What would overturn this.** A model that keeps training might learn to do
the whole thing in one pass, which would erase the effect. We checked:
between 110,000 and 235,000 training steps the pattern did not weaken, and
the one loop scores actually got worse (chain length 6 fell from 66% to
60%). The model is committing harder to using its loops, not training its
way out of needing them. Final numbers, on three independent repeats,
arrive within about six hours.

## 2. Why the earlier version measured nothing

This is the uncomfortable part, and it is worth reading, because the fault
was ours and the way it was found matters more than the fault itself.

The previous version of this test scored 100% everywhere, at every number
of loops. We had been reading that as "the model is good at this". It
actually meant the test could not tell us anything: whatever the truth had
been, the answer would have come out 100%. Three separate causes, all found
by auditing the data rather than by any test failing.

**The scoring floor was wrong.** Questions could ask about colour, shape or
size. Size has only two possible answers, so one question in three was a
coin flip. We had been describing the task's difficulty as "1 chance in
13", when a model that knew nothing would in fact score 29%. Some earlier
runs we had recorded as showing partial progress were at or below pure
guessing.

**The reasoning could be skipped.** A model that ignored the chain of
relations entirely, and simply guessed the most common answer among the
objects it could reach, scored 62% on the longest chains. Nearly one
question in five had only one possible answer no matter what the chain
said. The pictures were too sparse for the chain to matter.

**The model never needed to loop.** A model of this shape does a fixed
amount of work before it starts looping at all. Ours did enough of that
work to walk a four step chain in a single pass, and our chains stopped at
four. So the number of loops was never the limiting factor. It could not
have been.

All three are fixed: size is no longer asked about, the pictures have more
objects, and the chains now run to six steps. The measured effect is that
skipping the reasoning now scores 44% instead of 62%, and questions with a
forced answer fell from 19% to under 1%.

**The cost.** Six completed training runs no longer tell us anything and
have been formally retired. That is roughly two days of computer time.

**How it was found.** Not by a failing test. Every test we had was checking
that the questions were well formed, and they were. The fault was that the
difficulty was not where the design said it was, which no test was asking
about. There are now tests that ask exactly that, and they fail if either
problem returns.

## 3. A second finding, possibly the more interesting one

Our other task family asks the model to track a sequence of transformations
applied to a shape. There are two independent kinds: **turning and
flipping** the shape, and **permuting its three colours**. Both are
genuinely order dependent, meaning you cannot get the right answer by
counting which operations happened, you have to track the order they came
in.

After a million training steps, the model gets the turning and flipping
**exactly right, every time**, and the colour permutation **completely
wrong, at pure chance**. Not partly wrong. Its score on the colour half is
indistinguishable from guessing, to four decimal places, on two independent
training runs.

Three things make this solid rather than a curiosity:

1. **It is genuinely doing sequential reasoning on the half it gets right.**
   A shortcut exists that ignores the order of the operations, and the best
   possible score using that shortcut is 81%. The model scores 100%, so it
   is not taking the shortcut.
2. **It can see the colours perfectly well.** On single step questions it
   reads the colour permutation with 100% accuracy. So this is not a
   problem with the images.
3. **Both training runs agree to four decimal places** from different
   random starting points. This is a stable end state, not variance.

We initially recorded this finding backwards, reporting that the model had
learned the "order independent" part of the problem. It had in fact learned
the order dependent part of one half, which is the opposite conclusion and a
considerably better one for the project, since it is direct evidence that
these models do sequential reasoning rather than avoid it. The number that
disproved our first reading was already sitting in our own results table,
and we had not thought to check it against the alternative.

**Following it up.** We are now training the model on each half separately,
to separate two explanations: is the colour half simply hard, or is it
merely neglected because the other half is easier and gets learned first?
Early results, about a quarter of the way through, are striking. Trained on
the turning and flipping alone, the model is learning. Trained on the
colour permutation alone, with nothing else to distract it, it sits at
exactly chance and has learned nothing at all. That points toward the
colour half being genuinely hard rather than merely neglected, which is the
more interesting of the two answers. Final results in about twelve hours.

## 4. Where the project stands

Against the sixteen week plan we are roughly three weeks ahead of schedule.
Foundations, data generation, the model, the training system, the baselines
and the first validity gate are all complete and tested. Twenty one
training runs finished, seven running.

Ahead on the calendar, thinner on results than the run count suggests, since
six of those finished runs are the retired ones.

**The honest risk.** Of our three task families, only one can currently
answer the project's main question. The transformation family cannot,
because the model does not solve it at all, so there is no "number of loops
needed" to measure. The third family is deliberately the control and is
meant to show no effect. So the main hypothesis now rests entirely on the
family described in section 1, and the project's next formal checkpoint
depends on those same runs. If they come back flat, the main question loses
its route, and the paper would have to lead with the finding in section 3
instead.

The current evidence says they will not come back flat. But one live path
where we had planned for two is worth knowing about now rather than in
October.
